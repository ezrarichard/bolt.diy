# Builders Sales & Business Development Playbook

## Purpose

This handbook helps the internal team sell software products that are built with Builders.

Important distinction:

- `Builders` is our internal AI workspace for product engineering.
- We do **not** sell Builders itself.
- We sell the websites, software products, automations, portals, dashboards, and apps created using Builders.

This playbook is written for a business-focused team with limited software development knowledge. It explains what Builders can do, who to sell to, how to qualify opportunities, how to run meetings, how to prepare proposals, and how to position our work in the market.

## 1. Introduction

### What Builders Is

Builders is our in-house AI product development platform. It is designed to help us create software faster, with more structure, and with fewer handoffs than a traditional software delivery process.

Based on the current repository, Builders supports:

- AI-assisted product development in the browser
- Multiple LLM providers per prompt
- An integrated terminal for running commands
- Live preview of generated applications
- Git integration for importing and managing projects
- MCP integration for external tools and services
- Supabase integration for database-backed applications
- One-click deployment to Vercel, Netlify, and GitHub Pages
- Project templates for common frontend and full-stack starting points
- Electron desktop usage
- File locking and diff visualization to reduce conflicts

### Mission

Our mission is to turn customer requirements into working software with speed, clarity, and practical business value.

### Vision

We want Builders to become the core engine for delivering software products across:

- Coimbatore
- Tamil Nadu
- Kerala
- Karnataka
- Andhra Pradesh
- Telangana
- South India
- Eventually pan India

### How We Work

The general workflow is:

1. Speak with the customer in business language.
2. Understand the problem, users, process, and urgency.
3. Translate the requirement into structured scope.
4. Use Builders to create the software product.
5. Review, test, refine, and deploy.
6. Support the customer after launch.

### How We Differ From Traditional Software Companies

Traditional agencies often depend heavily on manual coding, long back-and-forth cycles, and large delivery overhead.

Builders changes that model by helping us:

- Start faster from templates and starter projects
- Use AI to accelerate product creation
- Work across many product types using the same platform
- Preview and validate outputs early
- Deploy quickly to modern hosting platforms
- Support many LLMs instead of being locked into one provider

The business benefit is not just speed. It is also better consistency, easier iteration, and the ability to respond to customer needs with less friction.

## 5. Customer Meeting Guide

### Goal of the First Meeting

The first meeting is not to sell software immediately.

Its job is to:

- Understand the business
- Identify the real problem
- Learn the process
- Confirm urgency
- Decide whether there is a fit

### How The First Meeting Should Feel

It should feel like a business conversation, not a technical interview.

Use language such as:

- “How do you handle this today?”
- “Where does this process slow down?”
- “Who uses it?”
- “What happens when there is a mistake?”
- “What would success look like?”

Avoid heavy technical words unless the customer uses them first.

### Questions To Ask

#### Business Context

- What does your business do?
- What service or product do you sell?
- How do customers currently come to you?
- What is the main business goal this year?

#### Current Process

- How is this work handled today?
- Is it done in Excel, WhatsApp, paper, or software?
- Who is involved in the process?
- What steps happen from start to finish?

#### Pain Points

- What is the biggest problem today?
- Where do delays happen?
- What gets missed or repeated?
- What causes mistakes?
- What is manual that should not be manual?

#### Users

- Who will use the software?
- How many people will use it?
- Will this be used by staff, customers, vendors, or management?
- Do users work in one office or multiple locations?

#### Data and Reporting

- What data do you need to store?
- What reports do you need daily, weekly, or monthly?
- What decisions do you make from this data?

#### Approvals and Controls

- Does anything require approval?
- Who can edit what?
- Do you need permissions by role?

#### Channels and Devices

- Will people use desktop, mobile, or both?
- Do users need access on the go?
- Do you need a customer-facing portal?

#### Integrations

- Do you already use accounting, SMS, email, payment, ERP, or CRM software?
- Do you need to connect with those tools?

#### Budget and Timeline

- When do you want this to go live?
- Is there a fixed budget range?
- Is this urgent or exploratory?

### How To Build Trust

- Listen more than you speak
- Repeat their problem in simple words
- Show that you understand the business, not just the technology
- Avoid promising everything
- Be honest about scope, timeline, and dependencies
- Give examples of similar workflows or products

## 7. Proposal Preparation

A good proposal turns the conversation into a clear commercial plan.

### Proposal Sections

#### 1. Scope

Define exactly what will be built.

Include:

- Main modules
- User roles
- Workflows
- Reports
- Integrations
- Platforms supported

#### 2. Deliverables

List the actual outputs.

Examples:

- Website
- Admin panel
- Mobile-responsive interface
- Dashboard
- Reports
- Deployment
- Documentation

#### 3. Timeline

Split the work into phases.

Example:

- Discovery
- UI/UX and planning
- Development
- Testing
- Deployment
- Handover

#### 4. Assumptions

State what the pricing and timeline depend on.

Examples:

- Customer provides content on time
- Customer approves scope within the agreed window
- Third-party integrations remain available

#### 5. Exclusions

State what is not included.

Examples:

- Data entry
- Third-party license costs
- Paid APIs
- Custom hardware
- Additional modules not listed in scope

#### 6. Pricing

Break pricing into:

- Discovery fee, if applicable
- Development cost
- Deployment cost
- Support cost
- Annual maintenance

#### 7. Payment Milestones

Common structure:

- Advance payment
- Mid-project milestone
- Pre-launch milestone
- Final handover payment

#### 8. Support and Maintenance

Clarify:

- Support window
- Bug-fix period
- Maintenance scope
- Response expectations
- Hosting responsibilities

#### 9. Change Requests

Explain how extra work will be handled.

Include:

- Scope change process
- Additional cost
- Impact on timeline
- Approval requirement

### Proposal Principles

- Keep it simple
- Be specific
- Avoid vague promises
- Align with business outcomes
- Use plain language

## 10. Handling Objections

### “Too costly”

Response approach:

- Acknowledge the concern
- Reframe around business value
- Explain what is included
- Offer phased delivery if needed

Example:

“I understand the investment is important. The price reflects the time, design, testing, and business logic needed to build something reliable. If helpful, we can also split this into phases so you can start with the most important parts first.”

### “Need more time”

Response approach:

- Respect the pace
- Leave room for follow-up
- Offer a simpler next step

Example:

“Of course. I’ll send a short summary and we can reconnect after you review it internally. If it helps, I can also prepare a smaller phased option.”

### “Already have software”

Response approach:

- Ask what is missing
- Focus on gaps and pain points
- Position as improvement, not replacement

Example:

“That’s good. Many customers already have a system but still struggle with reporting, speed, or manual work around it. If you’re open to it, we can look at whether a new module, integration, or replacement is actually the better fit.”

### “Competitor is cheaper”

Response approach:

- Compare scope, support, and quality
- Avoid defensive pricing
- Explain long-term value

Example:

“Cheaper options can be useful for smaller needs, but the key question is whether the solution will remain usable and scalable for your business. We focus on reliability, clarity, and support, not just the lowest upfront cost.”

### “Need approval”

Response approach:

- Provide a summary for internal review
- Make it easy for them to present internally

Example:

“That makes sense. I can share a one-page summary with scope, benefits, timeline, and investment so you can review it with your team.”

### “No budget”

Response approach:

- Explore phased scope
- Suggest a minimum viable version
- Keep the relationship alive

Example:

“If the full project is not possible right now, we can look at a smaller version that solves the most urgent problem first.”

### “Need customizations”

Response approach:

- Clarify what is essential
- Separate must-have from optional
- Explain impact on cost and timeline

Example:

“Absolutely. Let’s separate the core requirements from the nice-to-haves so we can keep the first version focused and manageable.”

## 19. What Not To Say

Avoid overpromising.

- Do not say Builders can instantly solve every problem
- Do not promise features before understanding requirements
- Do not describe future capabilities as current ones
- Do not make the conversation too technical
- Do not position price alone as the main reason to buy

## 20. Internal Rules

- Sell the product outcome, not the platform
- Keep discussions business-led
- Use Builders to speed delivery, not to skip planning
- Separate current capability from future roadmap
- Write down requirements before quoting
- Use phased delivery where needed
- Protect margin on complex work
- Build long-term customer trust
